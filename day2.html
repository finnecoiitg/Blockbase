Day 2

Ethereum and Smart Contracts

Bitcoin solved one specific problem: how to transfer value between two people, anywhere in the world, without trusting anyone in the middle. It does that one thing extremely well. But it was never designed to do much else.

In late 2013, a 19-year-old named Vitalik Buterin started thinking about what it would mean to build a blockchain that could run arbitrary code — not just transfer a currency, but execute any program. He'd been writing about Bitcoin for a year, had read everything, and had watched developers try to build applications on top of it. Each time, they hit the same ceiling. Bitcoin's scripting language is deliberately limited. You can write simple conditions for spending coins. You can't build a loan protocol or a voting system or an exchange. The foundation was too narrow.

His proposal: a new blockchain with a general-purpose programming environment built in. Not a blockchain for currency. A blockchain for computation.

Where Ethereum came from

Vitalik Buterin was born in 1994 in Russia, moved to Canada as a child. He was prodigiously good at mathematics — reportedly three times faster at mental arithmetic than his peers by age four.

As a teenager he played World of Warcraft obsessively. One day, Blizzard patched the game and removed the damage component from his favourite warlock skill — Siphon Life. Vitalik was devastated. He later wrote: "I happily played World of Warcraft during 2007–2010, but one day Blizzard removed the damage component from my warlock's Siphon Life spell... I cried myself to sleep, and on that day I realised what horrors centralised services can bring." A trivial grievance. But it planted something: the instinct that centralised systems — where one company can unilaterally change something that matters to you — are a problem.

In 2011, at 17, he discovered Bitcoin through his father. He had no money to buy it, so he started writing articles for a cryptocurrency blog at 5 BTC per article. By late 2011, he'd co-founded Bitcoin Magazine — the first serious publication dedicated to cryptocurrency — and was its lead writer. The following year he won a Thiel Fellowship: a $100,000 grant to pursue a project instead of finishing college. He dropped out of the University of Waterloo.

He spent the next year travelling the world visiting every notable crypto project. He talked to developers, read whitepapers, understood every approach being tried. And he noticed the same pattern everywhere: each project was building one specific application on a blockchain — a decentralised domain name system, a prediction market, a storage network. Each was rebuilding its own consensus layer from scratch just to do one thing. The insight: what if instead of application-specific blockchains, there was a general-purpose blockchain that anyone could build any application on?

In November 2013, he wrote the whitepaper. He was 19. He took it to Bitcoin developers, who told him it wasn't necessary — use Bitcoin's scripting language. (Bitcoin's script is deliberately not Turing-complete: it can express conditions for spending coins, but it cannot run arbitrary programs.) He decided to build it independently.

Gavin Wood wrote the technical specification — the Yellow Paper — and invented Solidity, the language you'll start writing in Day 3. Charles Hoskinson handled early operations before a falling-out; he went on to found Cardano. Joseph Lubin co-founded the Ethereum Foundation and later ConsenSys, the company now behind MetaMask.

Key moments in Ethereum's history since then:

  • January 2014 — Ethereum announced publicly at the Bitcoin conference in Miami
  • July–August 2014 — The crowdsale raises 31,591 BTC (~$18 million at the time) — one of the first major token sales ever conducted
  • July 30, 2015 — Mainnet launches. The team named it "Frontier" — deliberately framing it as a frontier, not a finished product.
  • June 2016 — The DAO hack: ~$60 million in ETH stolen from a popular smart contract via a re-entrancy vulnerability. The community controversially hard-forked to reverse the theft, splitting the chain into Ethereum (ETH) and Ethereum Classic (ETC) — the unforked chain that refused the reversal on the grounds that "code is law."
  • 2020 — DeFi Summer: Uniswap, Compound, and Aave collectively handle billions in daily volume on Ethereum smart contracts. Decentralised Finance becomes real.
  • September 15, 2022 — The Merge: Ethereum switches from Proof of Work to Proof of Stake. Energy use drops 99.95% overnight. One of the most complex live software migrations ever executed on infrastructure holding hundreds of billions of dollars. (Task 3 covers this in detail.)
  • 2024 onwards — Layer 2 networks (Arbitrum, Optimism, Base, zkSync) scale Ethereum's throughput massively. The ecosystem is now a multi-chain world built on Ethereum's security.

Smart contracts

The term predates Ethereum by over two decades. A computer scientist named Nick Szabo described the concept in 1994: a contract whose terms are directly written as code, executing automatically when conditions are met, without needing a lawyer, court, or any other enforcement mechanism.

His classic example was a vending machine. You insert the correct amount of money; the machine releases a product. No human needs to verify the payment or hand you the item. The logic is built into the machine. Trust is replaced by mechanism.

Szabo's idea was mostly theoretical for years — digital systems couldn't guarantee that code would execute as written, because they all ran on servers owned and controlled by someone. A database administrator could alter the logic. A company could shut the system down. The trust problem was just moved, not eliminated.

Ethereum solved this. A smart contract on Ethereum is a program deployed to the blockchain. Once it's deployed, no one can alter it — not the developer who wrote it, not Vitalik, not any government. The code runs exactly as written, every time, on thousands of nodes simultaneously. The execution is transparent and verifiable by anyone.

In practice, smart contracts power most of what you'll interact with in Web3: token standards, decentralised exchanges, lending protocols, NFT marketplaces, stablecoins. All of it is running on contract code that no one can unilaterally alter.

The EVM

When a smart contract is deployed to Ethereum, it gets compiled down to bytecode — a low-level instruction set that runs inside the Ethereum Virtual Machine, or EVM.

The EVM is not a physical machine. It's a specification — a precise set of rules describing how code executes — and every Ethereum node runs a software implementation of those rules. When a transaction triggers a contract, every node executes the same bytecode independently and arrives at the same result. Ten thousand computers in fifty countries, all running the same code, all reaching the same answer. If any of them produces a different result, the rest of the network rejects it.

That's what "trustless execution" actually means in practice. Not that the software is perfect or that no one can make mistakes — but that no single party controls whether the code runs or what it does. The outcome is enforced by consensus across thousands of independent machines, not by any company's promise.

The EVM is Turing-complete, meaning it can run any computable program given enough resources. Bitcoin's scripting language deliberately is not — it can only express simple spending conditions. That single design choice is what separates the two chains in terms of what you can build on them.

One important consequence: Polygon, Arbitrum, Optimism, Base, and most other major chains use the same EVM specification. A contract written for Ethereum mainnet can usually be deployed on these chains unchanged. Whatever you learn here applies across most of the Web3 ecosystem, not just Ethereum.

Two types of account

Ethereum has two distinct types of account, and understanding the difference matters for everything that follows.

Externally Owned Account (EOA)
Controlled by a private key. This is what you create when you set up MetaMask. An EOA can hold ETH and tokens, send transactions, and interact with contracts. It has no associated code. The only way to initiate a transaction on Ethereum is through an EOA — contracts cannot initiate transactions on their own.
Contract Account
Created by deploying a smart contract to the network. It has an address, can hold ETH and tokens, and executes code when called. It has no private key — it's controlled entirely by the logic written into it at deployment. It cannot act independently; it can only respond when an EOA (or another contract) sends it a transaction.

Both types of account have a 42-character address starting with 0x — for example, 0x71C7656EC7ab88b098defB751B7401B5f6d8976F. From an address alone, you can't tell whether it belongs to a person or a contract. You'd need to query the network.

Transactions

Every state change on Ethereum happens through a transaction. Sending ETH, calling a smart contract, deploying code — all of it is a transaction. Each one contains the following fields:

From
The sender's address. Always an EOA — contracts cannot initiate transactions on their own.
To
The recipient's address — another wallet, or a contract. If this field is empty, you're deploying a new contract.
Value
The amount of ETH to send, denominated in wei. Can be zero — many contract calls send no ETH, they just invoke a function.
Data
For contract calls, this encodes the function being called and its arguments. For a plain ETH transfer, this field is empty.
Gas limit
The maximum gas you're willing to spend. If the transaction uses less, the remainder is refunded. If execution runs out of gas mid-way, the transaction fails and state is rolled back — but the gas already consumed is not refunded.
Nonce
A counter: the number of transactions sent from this address so far. Prevents replay attacks — a transaction reusing a nonce you've already used is rejected by the network.
Signature
Cryptographic proof that the sender owns the private key for the From address. Without a valid signature, the transaction is rejected. No one else can submit a transaction on your behalf.

You'll see every one of these fields when you inspect a transaction on Etherscan. Task 2 walks through what they look like on a real transaction.

Ether

Ether (ETH) is the native currency of the Ethereum network. It serves two purposes: it's a transferable asset, and it's what you pay to use the network's computational resources (called gas — covered in Task 2).

ETH is denominated in very small units. The smallest unit is a wei, named after Wei Dai, a cryptographer who published a proposal for digital money called b-money in 1998 — work that influenced Satoshi directly. One ETH is 1018 wei.

wei
The smallest unit. 1 ETH = 1,000,000,000,000,000,000 wei.
gwei
109 wei. This is the unit you'll see most often when looking at gas prices. "Gas costs 15 gwei" means 15 × 109 wei per unit of gas.
ETH
1018 wei. What prices, portfolio values, and most discussions are denominated in.

Unlike Bitcoin, Ethereum has no hard supply cap. New ETH is issued as validator rewards. However, since August 2021 (EIP-1559), a portion of every transaction fee is permanently burned — destroyed forever. During periods of high network activity, the burn rate exceeds issuance, making ETH deflationary. Whether that makes it a better or worse monetary asset than Bitcoin is a debate that has filled entire conferences.

dApps and the Web3 stack

A dApp — decentralised application — is an application whose core logic runs on a smart contract rather than a private company's server. 

Traditional app:   User → Browser → Company Server → Company Database

dApp:              User → Browser → ethers.js → MetaMask → Smart Contract

The key difference is the backend. A smart contract on a public blockchain is readable by anyone, verifiable by anyone, and — once deployed — modifiable by no one. No company can shut it down, change the rules, or deny your access. You're interacting directly with code, not with a company's promise to behave honestly.

Some real dApps running entirely on Ethereum smart contracts:

  • Uniswap — swap any ERC-20 token for any other with no account, no KYC, no company intermediary. Processes billions in daily volume. No employees handle the trades.
  • Aave — lend and borrow crypto. Interest rates set algorithmically by supply and demand on-chain.
  • ENS (Ethereum Name Service) — buy a .eth domain (e.g. vitalik.eth) that maps to your Ethereum address, managed entirely by a contract.
  • MakerDAO — issues DAI, a stablecoin pegged to USD via smart contract collateral — no company's bank account involved.
  • OpenSea — an NFT marketplace where the smart contract handles escrow and ownership transfer.

ERC standards

Because anyone can deploy tokens on Ethereum, there needs to be an agreed-upon interface so wallets, exchanges, and dApps can work with any token without writing custom code for each one. That's what ERC standards are — Ethereum Request for Comments, the process by which interface standards get defined and adopted across the ecosystem.

ERC-20
Fungible tokens — every unit is identical, like coins. USDC, DAI, LINK, and UNI are all ERC-20 tokens. Standard functions include transfer(), balanceOf(), and approve(). If a wallet supports ERC-20, it can display any ERC-20 token without custom integration.
ERC-721
Non-fungible tokens — each token has a unique ID and is not interchangeable one-for-one with another. CryptoPunks and Bored Apes are ERC-721. Used for digital art, collectibles, and in-game items where uniqueness matters.
ERC-1155
A multi-token standard supporting both fungible and non-fungible tokens in a single contract. Common in gaming — interchangeable in-game currency and unique legendary items can coexist in the same contract, reducing deployment overhead.

You won't write these from scratch. Days 5 and 6 use OpenZeppelin's audited implementations. But understanding what they are and why they exist matters before you start using them.

Before you can deploy a contract or send a transaction, two things need to be clear: why every operation on Ethereum costs something, and what a wallet actually is. Both are misunderstood in ways that cause real problems for people.

Gas

Consider what it actually means to run a program on a network of ten thousand independent computers. Every time someone calls a function on a smart contract, that function executes on every node simultaneously. Add two numbers, read from storage, update a balance, call another contract — each operation burns real CPU time, replicated across the entire network. Something has to price that.

That something is gas. Gas is a unit of computation — not ETH itself, but a measure of how much work a given operation requires from the EVM. A simple ETH transfer costs exactly 21,000 gas. A more complex contract interaction — checking balances, updating state, emitting events — might cost 200,000. Writing to permanent on-chain storage, the most expensive EVM operation, costs 20,000 gas per 32-byte slot.

The total transaction fee you pay is:

fee = gas used × gas price (in gwei)

Example:
  Gas used:    21,000  (a simple ETH transfer)
  Gas price:   15 gwei
  Total fee:   21,000 × 15 gwei = 315,000 gwei = 0.000315 ETH

To build intuition for what different operations actually cost:

Operation Gas used At 20 gwei In ETH
Simple ETH transfer 21,000 420,000 gwei 0.00042 ETH
ERC-20 token transfer ~65,000 1,300,000 gwei 0.0013 ETH
Contract interaction ~100,000–200,000 2–4M gwei 0.002–0.004 ETH
Complex DeFi swap ~300,000 6,000,000 gwei 0.006 ETH

Gas also solves a problem that comes directly from the EVM being Turing-complete. Because you can write any program, you can accidentally — or deliberately — write an infinite loop. If computation were free, one transaction could spin forever and lock up the entire network. Gas is how the protocol enforces a ceiling: every transaction includes a gas limit, the maximum it's allowed to consume. Hit that limit before execution finishes and the transaction fails, all state changes are rolled back, and the gas spent up to that point is still charged. You paid for the work the network did, even though the result was failure.

How fees are priced — EIP-1559

Before August 2021, gas pricing was a blind auction. You guessed what other people were paying, added a margin, and hoped your transaction got in. During periods of congestion, users routinely overpaid by 10x because they were afraid of guessing too low and waiting hours for confirmation. It was a terrible user experience, and the unpredictability made it hard to build anything that required reliable cost estimates.

EIP-1559, implemented in August 2021, replaced the auction. Every block now has a base fee — a minimum price per gas unit — set algorithmically by the protocol itself, not by user bids. You pay the base fee plus an optional priority fee (tip) to the validator. The base fee is burned — sent to a zero address and permanently removed from supply. No one collects it.

Base fee
Set by the protocol. Rises when blocks are more than 50% full, falls when they're less than 50% full. This makes fee estimation far more predictable than the old auction model.
Priority fee (tip)
Paid to the validator. Incentivises validators to include your transaction rather than a competing one with the same base fee. During congestion, a higher tip gets you in sooner.
Max fee
The maximum per-gas price you're willing to pay in total. If base fee + priority fee is less than your max fee, you're refunded the difference. This lets you set a ceiling without overpaying.

Because the base fee adjusts automatically, fees spike during congestion (everyone competing for the same block space) and drop during quiet periods. Weekdays during US and European market hours tend to be expensive. Weekends and late-night UTC are cheaper. For test deployments, this doesn't matter — you'll be on a testnet with free ETH. For production, it does.

Try it etherscan.io/gastracker

Look at the current gas price in gwei. Check what a "standard" transfer costs in USD. Then use the formula above to verify: 21,000 gas × current base fee in gwei = fee in gwei → convert to ETH → convert to USD. The numbers should match the tracker's estimate.

Come back to this page at different times of day over the next few days and notice how much the base fee fluctuates. The range is typically 5–100 gwei depending on network load.

What a wallet is

The most common misconception in crypto: a wallet stores your coins. It doesn't. Your coins — your ETH, your tokens — exist as entries on the blockchain. They don't live in any app. They can't be moved to your wallet or out of it.

What a wallet stores is a private key: a randomly generated 256-bit number. That's all. The private key is what gives you the ability to sign transactions from your address. Whoever holds the private key controls the account. The wallet application is just software for managing and using that key.

Here's how the cryptography works:

Private key  →  (elliptic curve multiplication)  →  Public key
Public key   →  (Keccak-256 hash, last 20 bytes)   →  Address (0x...)

The derivation only goes one way. Given an address, you cannot recover the public key. Given a public key, you cannot recover the private key. Anyone can send ETH to your address — it's public. Only you can spend from it — because only you have the private key that proves ownership when signing a transaction.

Lose the private key, and you lose access to the account. Permanently. There is no customer support. There is no "forgot password." The blockchain doesn't know who you are — it only verifies signatures. Roughly 20% of all Bitcoin in existence is estimated to be permanently inaccessible due to lost keys.

Seed phrases

A 256-bit private key is a number like 0x4c0883a69102937d6231471b5dbb6e538eba2ef399a9a9bed4.... No one can memorise that. The seed phrase (also called a mnemonic or recovery phrase) is a human-readable encoding of the same information.

Under the BIP-39 standard, a 128-bit seed becomes 12 words; a 256-bit seed becomes 24 words. Each word maps to an entry in a fixed 2048-word list. The words carry the full entropy of the original key — writing them down in order is exactly equivalent to writing down the private key itself.

"witch collapse practice feed shame open despair creek road again ice least" — a valid BIP-39 seed phrase (do not use this one)

What this means in practice:

  • Anyone who reads your seed phrase has full access to your account. It doesn't matter that they don't have your phone or your MetaMask password. The words are the key.
  • Never enter your seed phrase into any website or app other than your wallet during initial setup. Any site asking for it is attempting to steal your funds.
  • Write it on paper and store it somewhere physically secure. A screenshot is a liability — phones get stolen, cloud backups get breached.
  • One seed phrase can generate many accounts (addresses), each with its own private key. MetaMask uses this — "Account 1", "Account 2" are all derived from the same seed via a deterministic path (BIP-44).

It's not a particularly elegant piece of software — there are better-designed alternatives — but it's what the ecosystem standardised on. Every blockchain faces the same fundamental question: when thousands of nodes each receive a batch of transactions, how do they agree on which are valid and in what order? No central authority decides. The nodes don't trust each other. And yet the network must converge on a single agreed-upon history.

The mechanism by which nodes reach this agreement is called consensus. Bitcoin and Ethereum have used different approaches to it, and understanding the difference matters — especially for Ethereum, which switched mechanisms in September 2022.

Proof of Work

Bitcoin's answer to consensus is computational competition. When it's time to add the next block, every miner on the network races to solve the same puzzle: find a number — called a nonce — such that when you combine it with the block's contents and hash everything together, the result starts with a specific number of zeros. 

Hash(block data + nonce) must start with 0000000000...

There's no clever shortcut. The only approach is brute force: try nonces one by one — 1, 2, 3, ..., 3,847,291 — billions of attempts per second on modern ASIC mining hardware — until one produces a valid hash. The number of required leading zeros is the difficulty target, which the network adjusts every 2,016 blocks (~2 weeks) to keep average block time around 10 minutes regardless of how much total mining power is on the network.

The first miner to find a valid nonce broadcasts the block to the network. Other nodes verify it in milliseconds — checking that Hash(block + nonce) starts with enough zeros is a single computation. This asymmetry is the core of Proof of Work: finding the solution is expensive; verifying it is essentially free.

The winning miner earns a block reward — newly minted Bitcoin. This is how new BTC enters circulation. Bitcoin's total supply is hardcoded at 21 million BTC. The reward halves every 210,000 blocks (~4 years): 50 BTC at launch, then 25, 12.5, 6.25, 3.125 (current, after the April 2024 halving). When all 21 million are mined — around 2140 — miners will earn transaction fees only.

Why this provides security: Rewriting blockchain history would require redoing all the computational work for every block you want to change — and finishing before the honest network adds more blocks on top. To successfully do this, you'd need to control more than half the network's total computing power — hence the 51% attack. For Bitcoin, the combined hash rate is currently in the hundreds of exahashes per second. Acquiring 51% of that would cost more money than most nations' GDP. For smaller chains with fewer miners, the same attack might cost a few hundred dollars per hour.

The energy problem: Proof of Work is intentionally wasteful. The computational difficulty is the security — the irreversibility of burned electricity is what makes the record trustworthy. Bitcoin's network currently consumes roughly as much electricity as a medium-sized country. Whether that tradeoff is acceptable is one of the more genuine debates in the space. It is the primary reason Ethereum moved away from PoW.

Proof of Stake

Ethereum's answer replaces computational competition with collateral. Instead of burning electricity to earn the right to propose a block, validators lock up 32 ETH as a deposit. This is called staking.

How block production works under Ethereum's Proof of Stake:

  • Every 12 seconds is a slot. Each slot, one validator is randomly selected — weighted by stake, using a verifiable random function built from validator contributions — to propose the next block.
  • A committee of other validators — hundreds of them — attests to the validity of the proposed block. An attestation is a vote: "I've verified this block and I agree it should be added."
  • When enough attestations accumulate (a supermajority), the block is added. The proposer and attesters earn small rewards in ETH.
  • After two rounds of voting (Casper FFG), a block achieves finality — it would be economically catastrophic to reverse it. Ethereum's finality takes about 12–15 minutes, compared to Bitcoin's probabilistic deepening over hours.

Slashing: If a validator tries to cheat — signing two different blocks for the same slot, or submitting votes that contradict their previous ones — the protocol detects it. The validator's staked ETH is slashed: a portion (up to all 32 ETH) is permanently destroyed by the protocol, and the validator is ejected from the network. The cost of cheating isn't wasted electricity — it's the direct financial destruction of the asset you staked.

This changes the security model. Under PoW, an attacker buys hardware and burns electricity. Under PoS, an attacker must first acquire an enormous amount of ETH (currently requiring tens of billions of dollars), stake it, and then risk losing it all to slashing if caught. The attack would also likely crash the price of the ETH being used to fund it. Attacking Ethereum PoS is economically self-destructive in a way that attacking a PoW chain isn't.

Side-by-side comparison

Proof of Work Proof of Stake
Who proposes blocks? Whoever solves the puzzle first (miners) Randomly selected validator (weighted by stake)
How to participate Buy ASIC mining hardware Lock up 32 ETH as collateral
Security mechanism Cost of electricity — irreversible physical expenditure Cost of staked ETH — slashing destroys it on detection
Punishment for cheating Wasted electricity + lost block reward Staked ETH slashed (destroyed by protocol)
Energy use Extremely high — Bitcoin ≈ medium-sized country's consumption ~99.95% less than PoW
Block time ~10 min (Bitcoin) — probabilistic 12 seconds (Ethereum) — fixed slots
Finality Probabilistic — deeper blocks = safer Economic finality in ~12–15 min
Used by Bitcoin (still PoW) Ethereum (since September 2022)

The Merge

On September 15, 2022, Ethereum executed one of the most technically audacious upgrades in software history. It switched a live network — holding hundreds of billions of dollars in assets, running thousands of dApps, processing millions of transactions — from one consensus mechanism to an entirely different one, with no downtime.

The transition was called The Merge because it merged two previously separate chains: the original Ethereum proof-of-work chain (handling all transaction execution) and the Beacon Chain — a parallel proof-of-stake chain that had been running since December 2020 as a live test of the validator set, without processing transactions. When The Merge happened, the execution layer detached from proof-of-work and attached to the Beacon Chain's consensus.

Block 15,537,393 was the last Proof of Work block, mined in the traditional way. Block 15,537,394 was the first Proof of Stake block, proposed by a validator. The transition happened at a pre-set terminal total difficulty — a cumulative hash value that the PoW chain was scheduled to hit, triggering the switch. The handoff was seamless.

Energy consumption dropped 99.95% overnight. Ethereum went from consuming as much electricity as a mid-sized country to consuming roughly as much as a large website.

Is PoS as secure as PoW?

Genuinely debated. Both arguments have merit and it's worth understanding each.

Bitcoin's camp argues: PoW's security is grounded in physics. When you mine a block, you burn real electricity that cannot be reclaimed. The cost is irreversible and external to the system. PoS security is denominated in ETH — a digital asset whose price fluctuates. Theoretical "nothing-at-stake" attacks — where validators vote on multiple chain branches because it costs them nothing — were a concern in early PoS designs.

Ethereum's camp argues: Slashing eliminates the "nothing at stake" problem — validators have enormous skin in the game. An attack on Ethereum PoS requires acquiring enough ETH to control a third of the validator set (roughly $30–50 billion at current prices), then risking that ETH being slashed on detection. The attacker destroys their own investment in the process.

Both systems are considered secure at their current scales. Whether the physical grounding of PoW is categorically superior to PoS's financial deterrence is a question that remains unresolved — and one you'll encounter in every serious discussion about blockchain architecture.
